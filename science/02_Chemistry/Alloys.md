> [!ABSTRACT] Introduction to Alloys
>
> Alloys are mixtures of at least one metal and another element. These alloys or mixtures are usually made to enhance properties that pure metals usually do not have.

In an **alloy**, atoms of different elements have **different sizes**. When you mix them into the lattice, these different-sized atoms distort the regular arrangement. This disruption acts like a structural speedbump, making it much harder for the layers of atoms to slide over each other. This generally makes alloys significantly stronger and more versatile than pure metals.

## Physical and Chemical Quirks

### 1. Melting Points

- **Pure Substances:** Have a precise, fixed, and definite melting point because every single bond holding the uniform lattice together requires the exact same amount of thermal kinetic energy to break.
- **Alloys (Mixtures):** Melt across a **range of temperatures**. Because the mixture is chaotic and contains different types of bonds, different parts of the structure break loose at different energy thresholds.

### 2. Enhanced Corrosion Resistance

Alloys can be engineered to resist environmental degradation better than pure elements.

- _Example:_ Iron rusts incredibly easily, but mixing it with chromium and nickel creates **Stainless Steel**, which forms a protective oxide layer that completely stops rusting.

---

## 🛠️ Common Real-World Examples

- **Steel:** Iron + Carbon (Stronger than pure iron, used for structural beams).
- **Brass:** Copper + Zinc (Malleable, low friction, used for musical instruments and plumbing).
- **Bronze:** Copper + Tin (Resists corrosion, hard, used for statues and coins).
