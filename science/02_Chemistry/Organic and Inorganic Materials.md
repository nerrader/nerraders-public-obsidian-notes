> [!info] Overview
>
> In the study of materials science and chemistry, substances are broadly categorized as either **organic** or **inorganic** based on their atomic structure and origin. Understanding this distinction is essential for predicting material behavior, such as thermal stability, reactivity, and electrical properties, and serves as the bridge between basic chemical theory and applied biochemistry.

## Organic

- Organic materials are chemical compounds characterized by the presence of **carbon atoms**, specifically bonded with **hydrogen** (C-H bonds).  
- These materials are primarily derived from **living organisms** or synthetic processes mimicking biological structures.  
- Examples include **wood, proteins, fats, DNA, and synthetic polymers** like plastics.
- Organic materials are typically **amorphous**, have **lower melting and boiling points**, are **combustible**, and generally have **poor electrical conductivity**.

## Inorganic

- Inorganic materials are compounds that **generally do not contain carbon** (with exceptions like carbon dioxide and carbonates) and lack carbon-hydrogen bonds. 
- They are typically sourced from **non-living entities** such as minerals, rocks, and metals. 
- Examples include **salts (e.g. sodium chloride), metals, glass, ceramics, and ammonia**.
- Contrast to organic materials, inorganic materials are often **crystalline**, have **high melting and boiling points**, are **non-combustible**, and are usually **soluble in water** with **good electrical conductivity** in solution.
